In simple terms, think of insurance as a **financial safety net**.

**Basics of Insurance**

Personal insurance is essentially an agreement between you and an insurance company. This agreement is called a policy, and if you have one, that makes you the policyholder. 

Examples of insurance companies in Singapore include Income Insurance, FWD, Etiqa Insurance, AIA, Prudential and Great Eastern.

**So, what actually happens when you buy personal insurance?**

In general, here is how it works:

- 1
    
    **Premium**: You pay the insurance company a set amount of money at regular intervals. This is known as your premium.
    
- 2
    
    **Coverage**: In return, the insurance company promises to protect you against specific risks, as detailed in your policy.
    
- 3
    
    **Payout**: If something unexpected happens (and it is covered under your policy), the company will pay out money to help you manage the financial impact.
    
- 4
    
    **Policy Expiry**: Once the policy ends, also known as expiry, you are no longer covered unless you renew it or buy a new one.

## Why we need insurance

Sure, being responsible is always a good idea. But life does not always go according to plan. Even when you are careful, unexpected events like accidents, illnesses, or sudden expenses can still happen. These can result in **significant financial burdens**.

That is where insurance comes in. It is a safety net that helps cushion the financial impact when the unexpected happens.

**That is the idea behind insurance: you pay a little over time, and in return, you are protected financially when life throws you a curveball.**


- A **deductible** is the initial amount you have to pay for your medical expenses before your health insurance makes a payout.
    
- **Co-payment** (or co-insurance) is how much you have to co-pay or split the cost with the insurer after you pay the deductible. It is usually expressed as a percentage. For example, if you have a co-insurance of 10%, you will pay 10% of the cost after the deductible.
    
- A policy **rider** may be added to an existing policy, which provides additional coverage.

To summarise the difference between Term insurance and Whole life insurance:

- **Term insurance** = pure protection, lower cost
    
- **Whole life insurance** = protection + savings, but more expensive


In general, insurance policies come with exclusions. These are specific situations or conditions where your insurer won’t pay out benefits.

**One common exclusion?**

**Pre-existing conditions.**

These are illnesses, disabilities or medical conditions you had **before applying** for the insurance.  

You will usually need to declare these when filling out your application. The insurer will then decide:

- Whether to cover the condition,
    
- Whether to charge a higher premium (this is known as a loading),
    
- Or whether to exclude it entirely from your coverage.
    

Sometimes, exclusions are also applied based on the **nature of your job** (e.g., high-risk occupations).

The important takeaway? Exclusions differ from policy to policy, so make sure you read the fine print and fully understand what you are covered for, and what you are not.

**(2) What is over-insurance?**

Over-insurance happens when you:

- Have multiple policies that cover the same risk, or
    
- Insure something for **more** than its actual value
    

At first glance, it might seem like a good thing. You are “extra protected,” right?

But in reality, over-insurance can lead to a problem known as **moral hazard.**

This is when a person might (even subconsciously) be tempted to take advantage of the insurance coverage for personal gain.

For example, if a payout is more than the item is worth, someone could be incentivised to file a claim unnecessarily or even cause the loss on purpose.

![](https://sin.scorm.canvaslms.com/courses/sconeID/scone_prod.sha256_0214c6b44ff8075dc3e06da8b26a9b37ee0071c7510cffdd61da2ab865160548/0/scormcontent/assets/01gmsxxhep1sfgqe04yvznmcxx.jpg)

Imagine someone buys a personal electronics insurance plan that covers their laptop for $3,000, even though the laptop is only worth $1,500.

If they lose or damage the laptop, the payout would be more than the actual value of the laptop. In extreme cases, this might even tempt someone to intentionally damage or “lose” the item just to receive the higher payout.

There are many real-world cases where individuals repeatedly file for fictitious claims,  only to be caught intentionally staging losses.

Insurers investigate patterns like these closely, and **fraud has serious consequences**, including policy cancellation, legal action, and being blacklisted by insurers.

![](https://sin.scorm.canvaslms.com/courses/sconeID/scone_prod.sha256_0214c6b44ff8075dc3e06da8b26a9b37ee0071c7510cffdd61da2ab865160548/0/scormcontent/assets/R.jpg)

Furthermore, having duplicate coverage could also lead to **unnecessary wastage of premiums**.  

For example, if you have two accident plans and you incur a medical bill of $1,000, you can only claim up to $1,000 in total, even if you have more than one policy.

This is because accident plans reimburse medical bills on a “reimbursement basis”, meaning they only cover the actual expenses incurred, not more.

Therefore, holding multiple accident plans does not mean you can claim the same $1,000 from both insurers, as **you cannot be reimbursed more than what you actually paid**.

Therefore, it is **your responsibility** to review your policies and make sure you are not doubling up or going beyond what you truly need.

Over-insurance not only leads to unnecessary premium payments but can also tempt individuals into unethical behaviour, resulting in severe legal repercussions.

**Your insurance should match your actual needs, not more.**

**The goal of insurance is to be protected, not to profit.**
